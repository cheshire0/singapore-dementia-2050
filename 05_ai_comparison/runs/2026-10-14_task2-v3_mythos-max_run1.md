**Headline:** Holding the 2023 WiSE age-specific prevalence constant and applying it to the UN medium-variant population, I estimate about **137,000** people aged 60+ with dementia in **2030** and about **296,000** in **2050**, with plausible ranges of **110,000–165,000** and **200,000–350,000**. The working follows in the order you set.

## 1. Assumptions

1. **The UN counts at ages 60+ approximate the resident (citizen + PR) population.** The UN counts the whole de facto population, including non-residents, but non-residents are almost entirely of working age (work pass holders, dependants, students). At 60+ they are a small share, so the UN figures overstate residents by at most a few percent. The UN 2023 total for 60+ (1,070,429) is also close to what I recall of the DOS resident count for 2023 (roughly 1.05–1.08 million). The UN medium variant is used for 2030 and 2050 because no resident-only projection by age is available in the data.
2. **The WiSE 2023 band prevalences are representative of everyone in each band**, and the same rates apply to the small non-resident group.
3. **Central scenario: age-specific prevalence stays at the 2023 point estimates.** This is the standard convention in dementia projections (Alzheimer's Disease International, GBD), it is transparent, and the 2013–2023 decline is not statistically established (section 4).
4. **Age composition within each band does not change.** Necessary because only three bands are given; the bias is upward for the open-ended 85+ band (section 7).
5. **The 10/66 case definition remains the relevant one.** Counts are people meeting survey criteria, not diagnosed cases.
6. **For uncertainty propagation, the three band estimates are independent and roughly normal**, with SE = (upper − lower)/3.92. Independence holds because the bands are disjoint subsamples; normality is an approximation, and the published intervals are asymmetric (longer upper tails), which this ignores.
7. **Trend scenarios:** a "continued decline" scenario uses the average 2013–2023 relative decline (about 14% per decade) continued at a constant rate; a "no decline" scenario uses the 2013 rates, which lie inside the 2023 intervals and so remain plausible current values.
8. Intermediate calculations are unrounded; final figures are rounded to the nearest thousand.

## 2. Method and calculations

Count in year *t* = Σ (population in band, year *t*) × (2023 prevalence in band).

The 85+ band accounts for 36% of cases in 2023 and 54% in 2050. Crude prevalence rises from 9.0% to 14.0% with no change in age-specific risk.

## 3. Check against the published 2023 count

My 2023 figure (96,694) is 31% above the published 73,918. Decomposing:

- **Denominator.** Published count ÷ published prevalence = 73,918 / 0.088 = 839,977. The study's implied population base is about 840,000, against the UN's 1,070,429 (ratio 0.785).
- **Prevalence.** My crude rate is 9.03% vs the published 8.8% (ratio 0.974).
- Product: 0.785 × 0.974 = 0.765 = 73,918 / 96,694. About 90% of the gap is the denominator.

Why the denominator is smaller: (i) WiSE counts residents only, whereas the UN counts total population, but this is a small effect at 60+; (ii) more likely, the count comes from survey weights tied to a sampling frame built before fieldwork and possibly excluding parts of the population. The 60+ population grows about 4% a year, so a frame three to four years old is about 15% smaller. The same pattern appears in 2013: 51,934 / 0.10 = 519,340, which matches the resident 60+ count at the 2010 census (about 525,000, from memory) rather than the 2013 resident count (about 640,000). The small prevalence gap (9.0% vs 8.8%) comes from the UN age structure being slightly older than the survey's weighting frame, consistent with (ii), plus rounding of the band rates to one decimal.

Implication: the disagreement is in the denominator, not the rates, so the projections apply the age-specific rates to UN denominators for all three years. If a reader insists on anchoring to 73,918, the same growth factors give about 105,000 (2030) and 226,000 (2050). I would not report those, because the published base appears to be well below the actual 2023 resident population aged 60+.

## 4. Is the 2013–2023 change real?

- **Band by band:** every 2013 estimate lies inside the 2023 interval: 3.4 within 1.9–4.7; 21.6 within 13.2–24.5; 56.2 within 38.8–58.6. No band shows a significant change.
- **Overall:** no 2023 interval is given. Propagating the band intervals with the 2023 age weights gives an SE of about 0.85 points, so roughly 8.8 ± 1.7 (7.1–10.5), which includes 10.0. If the 2013 estimate has similar precision, the 1.2-point fall has an SE of about 1.2 points: z ≈ 1, p ≈ 0.3. Not significant.
- **Evidence for a real decline:** the direction and size are consistent across bands (−12%, −16%, −14% relative). The age-standardised decline is larger than the crude one: applying 2013 rates to the 2023 age structure gives 10.5%, against 9.0% with 2023 rates, a 14% fall despite an older population. This matches the roughly 13%-per-decade decline in dementia incidence reported from European and US cohorts, and the mechanisms (rising education, better vascular risk control) apply strongly in Singapore.
- **Alternative explanations:** sampling error alone suffices. Changes in response rates, informant availability, fieldwork timing (pandemic period), coverage of institutionalised residents, and survival differences could each shift prevalence by this much.

Judgement: a real decline is plausible and somewhat more likely than not, but two surveys of this size cannot establish it. I do not base the headline on its continuation; I use it to set the low scenario.

## 5. Prevalence after 2023 and the ranges

Forces pushing age-specific prevalence down: cohort effects. The people aged 75+ in 2050 were born 1965–1975 and have far more schooling than the 1938–1948 cohorts in that band now; hypertension control is better and smoking lower. Forces pushing it up: rising diabetes and obesity; longer survival with dementia (prevalence = incidence × duration); better detection; disease-modifying drugs that extend time lived with mild disease; and ageing within the 85+ band.

| Scenario | 2030 | 2050 |
|---|---|---|
| Constant 2023 rates (central) | 137,000 | 296,000 |
| Approximate 95% interval from sampling error, constant rates | 112,000–162,000 | 248,000–344,000 |
| All three bands at lower / upper CI bounds (outer limit) | 101,000 / 184,000 | 223,000 / 384,000 |
| Decline continues at 2013–23 pace (×0.86/decade → ×0.90 by 2030, ×0.67 by 2050) | 123,000 | 197,000 |
| No decline (2013 rates: 3.4%, 21.6%, 56.2%) | 160,000 | 344,000 |

Workings: sampling SEs by band are 0.71, 2.88 and 5.05 percentage points; SE of the total = √Σ(N × SE)² = 12,700 (2030) and 24,500 (2050). The 2013-rate scenario: 2030 = 30,600 + 72,844 + 56,325; 2050 = 42,299 + 116,946 + 184,781.

Range I would quote: **110,000–165,000 for 2030; 200,000–350,000 for 2050.** This combines the sampling interval with the trend scenarios. I exclude the outer limit because all three bands sitting at the same end of their intervals at once is improbable.

## 6. What I would report

"About 137,000 residents aged 60+ with dementia in 2030 (plausible range 110,000–165,000) and about 296,000 in 2050 (range 200,000–350,000), compared with about 97,000 in 2023 on the same basis: a 1.4-fold rise by 2030 and a 3-fold rise by 2050." For a less precise-looking headline, 140,000 and 300,000 are defensible roundings.

Reasons: the constant-rate method is standard, transparent and easy to update; the decline is not established; and the growth factors (×1.4, ×3.1) are robust to the denominator question, so they hold whichever 2023 baseline a reader prefers. I would also report the crude prevalence path (9.0% → 10.3% → 14.0%) to make clear that the increase comes from population ageing, not from rising individual risk, and I would state explicitly why the 2023 figure of 97,000 differs from the published 73,918.

## 7. Main limitations

1. **Denominator.** UN totals include non-residents and may differ from DOS resident counts; resident-only figures could be 2–4% lower (about 3,000–5,000 in 2030; 6,000–12,000 in 2050). The 2050 figures depend on UN mortality and migration assumptions and on future immigration policy.
2. **Imprecise rates.** The inputs come from a single survey of roughly 2,500 people. The 85+ rate has an interval of 38.8–58.6% and drives 54% of the 2050 total; each 1-point change in that rate moves the 2050 estimate by about 3,300.
3. **Constant-rate assumption.** Cohort effects, risk-factor trends, survival, detection and new therapies could all shift rates in either direction over 27 years.
4. **Coarse age bands.** Prevalence roughly doubles every 5–6 years of age above 80. If the 90+ share of the 85+ band rises by 2050, as improving survival implies, the band rate could rise by several points without any change in age-specific risk, adding perhaps 3–5% to the 2050 estimate.
5. **Case definition.** 10/66 criteria capture milder cases than clinical or DSM diagnoses. These counts are not diagnosed cases or service demand; many people counted will be undiagnosed.
6. **Survey coverage.** Representativeness depends on response rates and on whether institutionalised residents were covered; the data do not say. Non-response bias in a dementia survey usually leads to under-ascertainment.
7. **No stratification** by sex, ethnicity or education, so composition changes within bands are not captured.
8. **Unverified inputs.** I have not checked the UN or WiSE figures against current sources, and my recollection of DOS counts used in section 3 should be verified before publication.
9. The ranges are scenario-based, not formal prediction intervals.
